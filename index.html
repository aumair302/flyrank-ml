Content Review Prioritization

A decision-support model for ranking content pages for human review using observable search-performance signals.

March 2026 data slice 3.61M rows Logistic Regression Client-grouped validation
Abstract

From search signals to a review queue

This project asks whether observable search-performance signals — impressions, clicks, CTR, and average position — can be used to prioritize content pages for human review. Using a March 2026 slice of the FlyRank internship warehouse, I built a rule-based baseline and compared it against a Logistic Regression model trained on the same signals. The baseline reported a perfect F1 of 1.0, a result closely tied to how its target was constructed rather than evidence of a flawless rule. The Logistic Regression model measured an F1 of 0.9444 on a random split and 0.9432 under a more conservative client-grouped split. The output is a ranked, reason-coded review queue intended to support human decision-making, not to automate content changes or claim causal SEO impact.

01 · Question

Which pages should a human reviewer look at first, and why?

Content teams routinely have more pages than time to review. The practical question is not “what will rank higher on Google?” but which pages show observable search signals worth investigating.

Scope: This work does not attempt to model Google's ranking algorithm or prove that changing a page will cause more clicks, rankings, or traffic. It produces a ranked review queue with reason codes and suggested human actions.

03 · Methodology

Simple, interpretable, and audited

Features

GSC impressions, GSC clicks, GSC average position, and CTR.

CTR = clicks ÷ impressions, calculated where impressions are greater than zero.

Model

Logistic Regression was chosen as a simple and interpretable comparison against the rule-based baseline.

Target

A binary prioritization target representing the defined attention/opportunity category used in the earlier baseline.

Baseline

The Week-4 baseline is a rule-based prioritization system using search signals and reason codes. It reported an F1 of 1.0 on its original evaluation setup. Because the target and signals are closely related, that perfect score is potentially circular and is not treated as evidence of a flawless real-world system.

Validation design

Random split. A stratified train/test split produced the initial model estimate.
Client-grouped split. Records from the same client were kept together so evaluation tested generalization across clients rather than client-specific patterns.
Leakage checks. Future-window information, label-derived fields, future outcomes, and product flags were not used as model inputs.

Action-score logic

action_score = log1p(impressions) × (1 / max(avg_position, 1))

The queue uses this score to order review attention. It is a prioritization heuristic, not a probability of future SEO success.

05 · Limitations

Honest framing matters as much as the score

Temporal scope

The analysis uses a March 2026 slice rather than a long-term production evaluation.

Proxy target

The target is a decision-support label, not a causal business outcome.

Baseline artifact

The baseline's F1 of 1.0 is closely connected to target construction and should be interpreted cautiously.

Missing context

Search signals do not fully describe content quality, search intent, business importance, seasonality, or editorial effort.

Historical evaluation

The model was evaluated on historical warehouse data and is not presented as a production system.

Decision-support only

Results are observed and measured for prioritization, not proof of causal SEO impact.

09 · Conclusion

A measured prioritization aid, not a promise about search

The capstone demonstrates an end-to-end ML workflow: define a practical search-intelligence question, build a transparent baseline, train an interpretable model, audit validation by client, check for leakage, and turn the output into reason-coded recommendations. The Logistic Regression model measured F1 values of 0.9444 and 0.9432 across the two validation designs. The most important conclusion is not that the model can “predict Google,” but that observable search signals can be organized into a repeatable human-review workflow when the limitations are stated clearly.
